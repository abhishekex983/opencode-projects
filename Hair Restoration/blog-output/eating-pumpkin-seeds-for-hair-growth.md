# Eating Pumpkin Seeds for Hair Growth

## TL;DR

Eating pumpkin seeds for hair growth provides your body with zinc, beta-sitosterol, omega-6 fatty acids, and antioxidants — nutrients that research suggests support the hair growth cycle. One randomized, double-blind study found that participants who took 400 mg of pumpkin seed oil daily for 24 weeks experienced a 40% increase in hair count, compared to just 10% in the placebo group. While pumpkin seeds aren't a cure for genetic hair loss, adding them to your daily diet is a simple way to cover nutritional gaps that may contribute to thinning.

## What's Inside Pumpkin Seeds That Supports Hair Growth

Pumpkin seeds pack a surprising amount of hair-healthy nutrients into a small serving. Understanding what's inside them helps explain why they've become a staple in my diet.

**Zinc** is one of the most important minerals for hair health. Pumpkin seeds contain approximately 7.5 mg of zinc per 100 grams. Zinc plays a role in protein synthesis and cell division — both of which are necessary for hair growth. Research suggests that zinc deficiency can contribute to hair loss, and getting enough from food sources like pumpkin seeds is a practical way to cover your bases.

**Beta-sitosterol** is a plant sterol found in pumpkin seeds, with approximately 265 mg of phytosterols per 100 grams. From what I've read, beta-sitosterol may influence the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase, which is involved in the conversion of testosterone to DHT. While I'm not a medical professional and can't explain the science in detail, this mechanism is one reason pumpkin seeds are often discussed in the context of hair health.

<img src="https://thinhairgrowthguide.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Nutrients.png" alt="Key nutrients needed for hair growth including zinc, omega fatty acids, and antioxidants found in pumpkin seeds" />

**Omega-6 fatty acids**, particularly linoleic acid, are another component of pumpkin seeds. <a href="https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/pumpkin-seeds" target="_blank" rel="nofollow">Healthline notes that pumpkin seeds contain omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids</a>, which support overall scalp health and may help reduce inflammation around hair follicles.

**Vitamin E and antioxidants** round out the nutritional profile. Vitamin E acts as an antioxidant that helps protect cells from oxidative stress. One study I came across suggested that vitamin E supplementation improved hair growth in people with hair loss, though more research is needed to confirm these findings.

One clinical trial found that participants taking 400 mg of pumpkin seed oil daily for 24 weeks saw a 40% increase in hair count, while the placebo group only saw a 10% increase. Researchers also noted that self-rated improvement and satisfaction were significantly higher in the pumpkin seed oil group at 24 weeks. This isn't medical advice — it's one study, and individual results vary — but I found it encouraging when I was exploring my options.

Your dermatologist can help you understand whether pumpkin seeds or other nutrient-dense foods make sense for your specific situation. For me, knowing that these nutrients support hair health was enough to make eating pumpkin seeds a daily habit.

## How to Eat Pumpkin Seeds for Hair Growth

Once I committed to eating pumpkin seeds regularly, figuring out how to actually incorporate them into my diet was the easy part. Here's what's worked for me.

**As a snack.** The simplest approach is just eating them straight. I keep a bag of pumpkin seeds on hand and grab a handful whenever I'm having barley tea, coffee, or chai. They're crunchy, slightly nutty, and satisfying enough to curb boredom eating — which is usually when I reach for them.

**In oatmeal.** This is my favorite way to eat them. I mix pumpkin seeds into my morning oats along with walnuts and blueberries. The combination is genuinely delicious — the seeds add a nice crunch, and the walnuts and berries round out the flavor. It also gives me a solid dose of protein, omega-3s, zinc, and antioxidants in one meal.

**Raw vs roasted.** Recently, I learned that raw pumpkin seeds are much more nutritious than roasted pumpkin seeds, mainly because they're in their unprocessed form. Even though raw seeds are a little harder to chew, I think they're more effective. Roasting can reduce some of the delicate fatty acids and antioxidants, so if you can handle the texture, raw is the better option.

**How much to eat.** There's no specific dosage I follow — I just eat a handful whenever I feel like it. Research suggests that the benefits come from consistent, daily intake rather than occasional large amounts. Think of it as a long-term habit, not a quick fix.
